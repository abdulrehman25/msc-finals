### Comment 1

> Section 4.5 states that the session model is trained using 36,000 normal-labelled CSIC 2010 requests, while Tables 4 and 5 appear to evaluate it on all 61,065 requests, including those same samples. Is the session model therefore being evaluated partly on its training data? If so, the reported AUC of 0.97 may overestimate generalisation and directly affect the conclusions of Section 5.3. Please clarify whether training and evaluation samples are fully disjoint and, if not, repeat the CSIC 2010 evaluation using a proper held-out normal subset. This should be possible with the existing data and code.

**Response:** The reviewer is correct — this was a genuine leakage risk, not a false alarm. The session model was trained on the 36,000 normal-labelled CSIC 2010 requests, and Tables 4 and 5 then evaluated it on all 61,065 requests, which includes those same 36,000 training rows.

We repeated the evaluation on a proper held-out normal subset, as requested, rather than only clarifying the text. CSIC 2010's benign traffic all comes from a single client identity (a detail we did not previously state explicitly, and now do in Section 4.5), so a per-client split is not possible; instead we split it chronologically. The first 28,800 benign requests (80%) are used for training, pooled with Access Super Long Session 2000 (giving 6,154 session windows in total). The session model is then evaluated only on the remaining 7,200 benign requests plus all 25,065 attack requests — 32,265 rows, none of which was used in training. Both the original (uncorrected) and corrected session models are now evaluated on this same held-out set, so the before/after comparison in Table 4 is on identical, unseen traffic (Section 5.1, and the note under Table 4).

On the held-out split, the corrected model's AUC is 0.977, against 0.633 for the uncorrected model — both essentially unchanged from the original (contaminated) figures of 0.97 and 0.63. We also re-scored the corrected model in-sample, on the exact 30,800 rows it was trained on, and obtained AUC 0.975, almost identical to the 0.977 held-out figure. We report this comparison explicitly in the paper (Section 5.1): the closeness of the in-sample and held-out numbers indicates the improvement is not being driven by memorisation. CSIC 2010's benign traffic is generated by replaying a fixed application workflow, so held-out windows from the same client are statistically very similar to the training windows, which is why the AUC does not drop under a disjoint split. The methodological problem the reviewer identified was real and is now fixed, and it did not change the paper's headline conclusion — if anything the held-out AUC (0.977) is marginally higher than the original in-sample figure (0.975).

F1 does move more than AUC does, from 0.14/0.22 (original/corrected) on the full 61,065-row file to 0.11/0.12 on the 32,265-row held-out split. This is a sample-composition effect rather than evidence that the earlier number was inflated by leakage: the held-out split has a much higher attack-to-benign ratio (25,065:7,200) than the full file (25,065:36,000), and F1 is sensitive to class balance in a way AUC is not, so the two F1 figures are not directly comparable at face value. We now state this in Section 5.1.

Table 5 (branch ablation) and Table 6 (fusion sweep) have been updated to use the same held-out split, and every occurrence of "0.63 to 0.97" in the Abstract, Section 5.1, the Discussion, and the Conclusion has been updated to "0.63 to 0.98," with a pointer to the held-out protocol.

### Comment 2

> Table 6 shows an unusual pattern: F1 remains almost unchanged (0.14–0.15) across most fusion weights, while AUC increases steadily from 0.70 to 0.78 before reaching 0.97 for the session-only model. Why does changing the fusion weight substantially affect AUC but barely affect F1? A finer sweep, for example in increments of 0.1, using the already computed scores would clarify where this transition occurs and better support the claim that intermediate fusion never helps.

**Response:** We reran the sweep in steps of 0.1 (11 points instead of 5) on the corrected, held-out CSIC 2010 scores, using the already-computed per-request scores as suggested (Table 6). The finer sweep answers the question directly: F1, precision, and recall are flat across $w_c = 0.5$ to $0.9$ (F1 = 0.112 throughout), move only slightly between $w_c = 0.1$ and $0.4$, and jump only at the two extremes ($w_c = 1.0$ and $w_c = 0.0$).

The reason is a scale mismatch between the two branches' raw reconstruction-error scores, which we had not previously reported. At the 95th-percentile cut-off used for thresholding, the content branch's score is about 58, against about 1.1 for the session branch — a ratio of roughly 54:1, much wider than the roughly 5:1 gap at the median. Because fusion is an unweighted average of the two raw scores (Section 4.6), any $w_c \geq 0.5$ leaves the content branch's much larger tail value numerically dominating the fused score, so the *set* of requests that clear the fused threshold — and hence precision, recall, and F1 — barely changes across that range. AUC keeps moving smoothly across the same range because it depends on the full rank ordering of scores, not on which requests clear one threshold, so even a small, growing session-branch contribution continues to shift the relative order of mid-ranked requests. We have added this explanation to Section 5.3, together with the full 11-row table (AUC, F1, precision, recall, and FPR at each weight), and flagged in the Discussion (Section 6) that the current unweighted-average fusion is not well matched to the two branches' different score scales — a concrete limitation we had not previously identified. The finer sweep confirms, rather than weakens, the paper's claim: both AUC and F1 still reach their maximum only at full session weight, with no interior peak on either measure.

### Comment 4

> The thresholding procedure for the classical baselines in Table 8 is unclear. The proposed branches use a 95th-percentile threshold, but how were Random Forest, Isolation Forest, and One-Class SVM thresholded? This is important because FPR comparisons are not fully meaningful if methods operate at different decision thresholds. Please clarify the procedure and, if necessary, provide a matched-threshold comparison or explicitly acknowledge the difference.

**Response:** This was a genuine gap — the baseline methodology existed only in code, not in the manuscript. We added a new Methods subsection, "Classical Baseline Methods" (Section 4.8), stating the procedure precisely. All three baselines are fitted on an 80/20 split of each dataset (fixed seed) and evaluated only on their own 20% held-out fold. Random Forest is supervised (uses attack labels during fitting) and thresholded at the standard 0.5 predicted-probability cut-off, the natural choice for a probabilistic classifier rather than a percentile threshold. Isolation Forest and One-Class SVM are trained on benign-only rows of the training fold (no attack labels, matching our own branches' semi-supervised setting) and thresholded at the 95th percentile of the score the fitted model assigns to its *own training-fold* benign rows, applied unchanged to the held-out test fold — the same self-calibrated percentile convention used for our own branches elsewhere in the paper, computed without ever touching test-fold scores.

Rather than construct a fully matched-threshold comparison, which would require re-deriving a common operating point across methods whose outputs are not on a common scale (a bounded probability for Random Forest versus unbounded scores for the others), we have explicitly acknowledged the difference the reviewer raised: the classical baselines are each evaluated on a 20% held-out fold of a dataset, while our own branches are evaluated on the dataset's full evaluation file (or, for CSIC 2010, the held-out split from Comment 1), so the exact sample compared differs in size between the two sides of each row in Table 8, even though neither side is ever scored on its own training data. This is now stated in Section 4.8 and repeated at the point in Section 6 where the FPR comparison is made, rather than left implicit.

### Comment 5

> Several evaluated datasets are very small, while all reported results come from a single run without confidence intervals. Some comparisons are also quite close, such as AUC 0.60 versus 0.64 on Access Mixed 500. Since per-request scores are already saved, bootstrap confidence intervals for the main AUC and F1 results could be obtained without retraining the models. This is really worth doing before presenting the comparative conclusions of Section 5.5 as settled!!

**Response:** We agree, and this comment changed our conclusions more than any other. We computed 2,000-resample bootstrap 95% confidence intervals for AUC (all methods) and F1 (our own content/session/fused branches, at their reported fixed threshold), using the per-request scores already saved by `evaluate_file.py`, exactly as the reviewer suggested — no retraining was needed for our own branches. For the classical baselines, per-request scores were computed during evaluation but had not previously been persisted to disk; we added that (`scripts/plot_and_baselines.py`) and reran the baselines with the same fixed seed, which reproduced the original point estimates within rounding and additionally gave us the row-level scores needed for bootstrapping. The new script is `scripts/bootstrap_ci.py`; full results are in `artifacts/eval/bootstrap_ci.csv`, and the AUC intervals for the fused branch and all three baselines are now reported as a new table in the paper (Table 9).

The reviewer's specific example resolves cleanly: on Access Mixed 500, the fused branch's AUC is 0.598 [95% CI 0.550, 0.645] and One-Class SVM's is 0.634 [0.526, 0.739] — the intervals overlap substantially, so this 0.60-vs-0.64 difference is not statistically distinguishable at this sample size (the OCSVM test fold is only 100 rows). Extending the same check to every fused-vs-classical-baseline comparison in Table 8, only two of the seven datasets have non-overlapping intervals: CSIC 2010, where the fused branch's interval (0.763–0.778) sits clearly above both unsupervised baselines' (Isolation Forest 0.703–0.721, One-Class SVM 0.437–0.458); and Access Attacks 200, where Random Forest (0.769–0.995) and Isolation Forest (0.724–0.972) both sit clearly above the fused branch's (0.385–0.557). On the other five datasets, our previous text described the fused branch as "clearly beating," "split against," or "beaten by" specific baselines on the strength of point estimates alone; we have removed that language throughout Section 5.5, the Discussion, and the Conclusion, since the confidence intervals show those differences are not established at the sample sizes involved — several baseline test folds have as few as 24–160 rows. We no longer present the Section 5.5 comparisons as settled; we state directly which of them are and are not statistically supported. We also revised the "Fifth" limitation in Section 6, which previously stated flatly that no confidence intervals were computed; it now reports what the intervals show and which comparisons they do and do not support.

We did not extend the bootstrap to F1 for the classical baselines, since each baseline's threshold (0.5 probability for Random Forest; 95th percentile of its own training-fold scores for Isolation Forest and One-Class SVM, per Comment 4) is fixed by the procedure in Section 4.8, and bootstrapping F1 at a threshold computed from the original, non-resampled training fold is exactly what we also did for our own three branches, keeping the comparison consistent across methods.
