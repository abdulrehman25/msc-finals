# Response to Reviewer Comments (Reviewer 2, Second-Round Minor Comments)

Dear Editor and Reviewer,

Thank you for checking the previous revision against its own tables rather than taking the response letter's claims at face value — two of the five points below (Figure 1, and the missing F1 intervals) were places where the manuscript had not actually caught up with what we said we had done, and a third (the Table 9 note) was a genuine overgeneralisation past what the table itself supports. All five are fixed directly in `Article.tex`, which recompiles to 28 pages without errors or undefined references.

We reproduce each comment below, followed by our response.

---

### Comment 1

> Figure 1 does not appear to have been regenerated for this revision. Table 4 and the surrounding text in Section 5.1 report the corrected session model's AUC as 0.98 (0.977 to three decimal places) and F1 as 0.12, both measured on the 32,265 row held-out split, with the original model at AUC 0.63 and F1 0.11 on that same split. Figure 1, however, shows AUC moving from 0.63 to 0.97 and F1 moving from 0.14 to 0.22. Those are the numbers the response letter itself describes as coming from the earlier, full 61,065 row evaluation, before the held-out split was introduced to fix the leakage I raised in the previous round, not the corrected held-out numbers now in Table 4. As it stands, the one figure meant to illustrate the paper's headline result contradicts the table sitting right above it. This should be a quick fix: replot Figure 1 from the same held-out scores already used for Table 4, so the bars read 0.63 and 0.98 for AUC and 0.11 and 0.12 for F1.

**Response:** Confirmed and fixed. The figure is generated by `scripts/make_extra_paper_figures.py` from `artifacts/paper/tables/session_v1_vs_v2.csv`, and that CSV's CSIC 2010 rows had not been updated when the held-out split was introduced — they still held the original, full-61,065-row point estimates (AUC 0.63/0.97, F1 0.14/0.22), even though every table and every sentence around the figure had already been updated. We corrected the CSV's two CSIC 2010 rows to the exact values behind Table 4 (original: AUC 0.6329, F1 0.1090, on the 32,265-row held-out split; corrected: AUC 0.9767, F1 0.1209, same split) and regenerated the figure. Figure 1 now reads 0.63 → 0.98 for AUC and 0.11 → 0.12 for F1, matching Table 4 exactly. We checked the other three figures produced by the same script (fusion-sweep, threshold-transfer, baseline-comparison) against their source CSVs while we were in there; those were already current and did not need regeneration.

### Comment 2

> The note beneath Table 9 states that "only the CSIC 2010 and Access Attacks 200 comparisons are outside overlapping intervals" and that every other dataset's fused-versus-baseline difference sits within combined sampling uncertainty. Checking the table's own numbers, this is not quite right for Access Eval Mix 2000: the fused branch's interval is 0.665 to 0.717, and Random Forest's is 0.728 to 0.819, and these two do not overlap. So there is a third case where a fused-versus-baseline comparison is outside overlapping intervals, it is simply against the supervised baseline rather than an unsupervised one. Section 5.5 and the Conclusion both handle this correctly, since they restrict the "overlaps at least one baseline" language to the unsupervised comparisons (Isolation Forest, One-Class SVM); it is only the summary note under Table 9 that generalises past what the table supports. Please either narrow the note to say "unsupervised baseline," matching the wording already used in the Conclusion, or add the Access Eval Mix 2000 versus Random Forest non-overlap as a third exception. This is a one or two sentence correction, since the relevant interval is already in the table.

**Response:** Confirmed. We checked every fused-vs-Random-Forest pair in Table 9 and found the reviewer's arithmetic is exactly right, and that it generalises: Random Forest's interval sits outside the fused branch's on three of the seven datasets, not just Access Eval Mix 2000 — CSIC 2010 and Access Attacks 200 as well (both already covered by the note, but for the wrong reason: the note attributed the CSIC 2010 and Access Attacks 200 exceptions entirely to the unsupervised-baseline comparison, when for CSIC 2010 and Access Eval Mix 2000 the Random-Forest comparison is also non-overlapping). We took the reviewer's first option and narrowed the note to the unsupervised baselines specifically, matching the wording already used in Section 5.5 and the Conclusion, and then added the Random Forest picture as its own, separate sentence rather than silently dropping it: "Random Forest, trained with attack labels, is a separate comparison: its interval sits outside the fused branch's on three of the seven datasets (CSIC 2010, Access Eval Mix 2000, and Access Attacks 200), consistent with its supervised advantage, and overlaps on the remaining four." This is more complete than the minimal one-line fix suggested, but the underlying arithmetic error was worth stating precisely rather than papering over.

### Comment 3

> Table 9 reports bootstrap confidence intervals for AUC, which is a real improvement over the single-run point estimates used earlier. F1 is still reported everywhere in the paper, including the six to seven fold threshold-transfer improvement in Table 7 and Section 5.4, as a single-run number with no accompanying measure of uncertainty. The response letter for Comment 5 indicates that F1 confidence intervals were in fact computed for the content, session, and fused branches using the same saved per-request scores used for the AUC bootstrap, but I could not find these reported anywhere in the current manuscript text or tables. If that is correct, including at least the F1 intervals for the branch ablation (Table 5) and, ideally, for the threshold-transfer comparison (Table 7) would let readers judge whether the six to seven fold F1 improvement is as robust as the AUC comparisons now are. If the F1 bootstrap was ultimately left out for a specific reason, a short sentence explaining why would be enough. Either way, no new computation should be needed beyond what the response letter describes as already done.

**Response:** Confirmed — this was an oversight, not a deliberate omission; the F1 bootstrap was computed and written to `artifacts/eval/bootstrap_ci.csv` but never made it into a table. We added it to Table 5 (branch ablation) as requested at minimum, formatting each F1 cell as `point [lo, hi]` rather than adding three more columns to an already-wide table, using exactly the numbers already sitting in `bootstrap_ci.csv` — no new computation there.

For Table 7 (threshold transfer), the reviewer is right that "ideally" this should be included too, and on inspection it needed one small addition, not a re-description: the self-calibrated F1 column reuses the same bootstrap already in `bootstrap_ci.csv`, but the *transferred*-threshold F1 column needed its own bootstrap, since it uses a different, externally frozen threshold (calibrated once on CSIC 2010's benign rows, per Section 4.7) rather than each target dataset's own self-calibrated threshold. This is a direct extension of the existing method, not new experimental work: we wrote `scripts/bootstrap_transfer_f1.py`, which resamples each target dataset's already-saved scores exactly as the existing script does, but applies the fixed transferred threshold instead of recomputing a percentile per resample. We added both F1 intervals to Table 7. In every one of the six rows, the self-calibrated and transferred intervals do not overlap, so the six-to-seven-fold F1 improvement reported in Section 5.4 is not an artefact of sampling variance at these sample sizes — which is the answer the reviewer was checking for.

### Comment 4

> The abstract states that "fusion is not uniformly beneficial: on CSIC 2010, weighting fully toward the session branch outperforms every blended weight tested," which is accurate for the raw, unweighted-average fusion reported in Table 6, but the manuscript's own later analysis complicates this somewhat. Section 5.3 shows that normalising each branch's score against its own training-score distribution before averaging raises the fused AUC from 0.76 to 0.95, close to the session branch's own 0.98, and Section 6 is explicit that the flat region in Table 6 is a property of unweighted raw-score fusion specifically, not of combining the two branches in general. A reader who only sees the abstract would reasonably conclude that fusing the two branches does not help at all on this dataset, when the fuller finding is that a specific, simple fusion rule does not help, while a cheap, label-free fix largely does. I would suggest qualifying the relevant abstract sentence, for example by adding "under raw score averaging" or similar, so the headline claim matches the more careful treatment already given in Sections 5.3 and 6. This is a wording change only.

**Response:** Agreed, and implemented as suggested. The sentence now reads: "Fusion under raw score averaging is not uniformly beneficial: on CSIC 2010, weighting fully toward the session branch outperforms every blended raw-average weight tested, though normalizing each branch's score against its own training-score distribution before averaging largely closes this gap." This is a wording-only change, as the reviewer notes; no numbers in Section 5.3 or the Discussion needed to change, since they already stated this distinction correctly — only the abstract had generalised past it.

### Comment 5

> The Data Availability Statement offers the self-generated logs, trained models, scalers, and score outputs "upon reasonable request." Given how much of the reported analysis in this revision, the finer fusion sweep, the corrected baseline thresholding, the bootstrap intervals, was carried out by rerunning saved scripts against already-saved scores rather than by retraining from scratch, the authors evidently already have a reasonably complete and organised codebase. For a paper whose central claims rest heavily on exact reproducibility of scores and thresholds, depositing the analysis scripts and the saved per-request score files, together with pointers to the public CSIC 2010 and NASA sources, in a repository such as Zenodo or GitHub, with a permanent link added to Section 4.10 or the Data Availability Statement, would be a stronger form of availability for this journal than request-based access, and should not require any new work beyond uploading what already exists.

**Response:** Agreed with the underlying point. We have committed to depositing the codebase in this revision, and updated both locations the reviewer suggested: a sentence in Section 4.10 ("Implementation and Reproducibility") now states that we intend to deposit the full analysis codebase, saved per-request scores, trained model weights, and scalers in a public GitHub repository, archived via Zenodo for a permanent DOI, upon acceptance, rather than relying on request-based access alone; the Data Availability Statement has been updated to match, and now also explicitly names the NASA Kennedy Space Center HTTP logs (Internet Traffic Archive) used to train the content branch, alongside CSIC 2010, as public sources. We have not pushed a public repository or minted a Zenodo DOI at this stage of review, since we would rather commit to and describe the deposit accurately than publish an incomplete or not-yet-finalised snapshot while the manuscript may still change; the permanent link will be added in place of the "upon reasonable request" fallback as soon as the deposit is created, which we can do immediately upon acceptance.

---

All changes above are implemented directly in `Article.tex` (Figure 1 regenerated; Table 9 note corrected; Table 5 and Table 7 both now report F1 with bootstrap 95% CIs; the abstract's fusion sentence qualified; Section 4.10 and the Data Availability Statement updated), and the manuscript compiles without errors or undefined references (28 pages). The corrected source data and new script behind these changes (`artifacts/paper/tables/session_v1_vs_v2.csv`, `scripts/bootstrap_transfer_f1.py`, `artifacts/eval/bootstrap_transfer_f1.csv`) are included so the reviewer can re-run any of these checks directly.

We thank the reviewer for reading the revision against its own tables closely enough to catch a stale figure and an unreported computation that the response letter had claimed was already done; both are the kind of inconsistency that is easy to miss from the author's side and important to catch before publication.
